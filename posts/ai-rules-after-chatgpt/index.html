What universities wrote into their AI rules after ChatGPT

audit findings
I followed the academic integrity and AI guidance pages of 92 universities in four countries from 2021 to early 2026. The new AI rules said far more about the offense than about how a student could contest a finding.
Author

Yuxia Liang

Published

November 25, 2026

When ChatGPT became publicly available on November 30, 2022, universities had to say how much of a student’s written work could come from a machine. I wanted to see what they actually wrote down. Using dated snapshots from the Internet Archive, I reconstructed how the academic integrity and AI guidance pages of 92 universities in the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada read in each quarter from 2021 to early 2026. I removed menus and other navigation text, then scored every version with a word list of provisions defined in advance.

I’ll start with two limits. This is a reading of written rules, and it can’t show how any rule is applied. The word list also catches more than it should: in 40 random draws of flagged passages, 25 were genuine, and the rest were generic advice to cite sources, general talk about responsible use, or discussion of detection tools. Every provision count below is therefore an upper bound, most of all for permitted use, disclosure, and detector language.

What the new rules said

About half of the universities added AI language to the pages I followed, most within about a year. A provision counted only when it appeared within 400 characters of an explicit AI term such as generative AI or ChatGPT. The bare abbreviation AI did not count in the numbers below, because some integrity offices use it for academic integrity. The restrictive provisions are an outright prohibition, a detector’s output accepted as evidence, AI use framed as misconduct, and an attached sanction. The procedural ones, on the remedy side, are explicit permission, a route to disclose or cite AI use, an appeal pathway, and a protection that names multilingual writers.

On the pages I followed, the new rules said far more about the offense than about the remedy. Misconduct framing reached 38% of American institutions, 24% of British ones, 47% of Australian ones, and 32% of Canadian ones. Outright prohibition appeared at 6% to 26% of institutions and detector language at 6% to 21%. Permitted use reached 12% to 37% and a disclosure route 12% to 24%, and counted together, the four restrictive provisions outnumbered the four procedural ones in every country. The word list found an appeal pathway near an AI term at two institutions, one American and one Canadian, but on reading, neither was an appeal route written into the AI rule. Multilingual writers were named at one institution, on an American teaching page warning that some responses to AI may disadvantage English-language learners.

What surprised me was how little the appeal and protection counts changed when I added the linked policy documents the archive holds, up to three PDFs per university. The documents raised the restrictive and disclosure counts, but they added no appeal pathway that survived a reading and only one more mention of non-native English speakers.

I want to be fair to the universities here. Where an institution-wide appeal route already applies to any misconduct finding, as the regulator’s standards require in Australia, the counting rule deliberately leaves that standing language out, so the count isolates what the new AI rule added. Appeal language did appear somewhere on the same page at half of the British universities and a quarter of the Australian ones whose pages addressed AI, but never within 400 characters of an AI term. The finding concerns what the AI rule itself says. It doesn’t tell us whether due process exists elsewhere in the institution.

How far the wording traveled

I also wanted to know whether universities mostly borrowed from national neighbors or whether the wording spread across all four countries. Comparing the AI text of every pair of universities quarter by quarter, I found that the language grew more alike within countries and across them at about the same pace. I treat this part of the study as exploratory.

Pairs in the same country did end up somewhat more alike. Outside the United States, though, that edge was already there in the integrity pages before ChatGPT, so the AI rules seem to carry over whatever national character the host pages had. In the United States, about three-fifths of the edge comes from the kind of page the text sits on: 21 of the 22 American institutions with AI text keep it on a dedicated AI guidance page, against eight of the 27 in the other three countries.

I’m unsure what to make of the British pattern. The six British universities with AI text looked closer to American text than to Australian or Canadian text, but six is too few to call that a national pattern, and the closeness comes from the four longest British texts. With every text cut to the same length, British text is no closer to American text than to Australian text. Overall, the pattern looks to me like a shared vocabulary added to the pages universities already had, and I found no single template behind it, international or national.

Restriction and international enrollment

A 2023 study by Weixin Liang and colleagues found that AI detectors wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers far more often than essays by native speakers. If universities were fitting their rules to local risk, restriction should rise with their share of international students, which ranges from about 5% to 53% where it is published. In the United States, where the sample is large enough to test this, universities enrolling more international students did not write detectably more restrictive rules. In the United Kingdom and Australia I could only check a simple correlation, which pointed in opposite directions and was indistinguishable from zero in both. No institution-level series of these figures is published for Canada, so it sits out this part.

The permissive side looked a little different. More exposed American universities may have written somewhat more about permitted and disclosed use, and the Australian data, from a single point in time, are at most consistent with that. I read this cautiously. Most of the American estimate rests on the codes for permitted use and disclosure, which the study’s own checks found among the least reliable, and about a third comes from one page at the most exposed institution. The estimate is fragile: it is borderline in the full sample and sensitive to which universities are included.

What this can’t tell us

The sample is mostly research-intensive universities, seen only through the pages the Internet Archive preserved, and that preservation is uneven and thin for the United States. ChatGPT reached every university on the same date, so the broad spread of AI rules can’t be pinned on it alone. International enrollment is only a stand-in for the writers a detector misreads, since some international students are native English speakers and some domestic students write English as an additional language. The text also can’t show which intermediary carried the shared vocabulary, such as a regulator or a detection vendor.

Why this might matter for a student

I keep thinking about the student on the other end of an allegation. The AI rules in this sample named the offense, and some named a way to detect it, but next to the rule they did not say how to contest a finding, and they rarely mentioned the detector’s known error with multilingual writers. Where a route to appeal exists elsewhere, in an institution-wide code or in regulatory standards, the new rule did not carry it over. For a student facing an allegation of AI misuse, that means the way to contest a finding may be written down somewhere other than the AI rule.

For the people who write these pages, the article’s suggestion is modest: a page that names the offense could also restate, in the same place, the routes to disclosed use and to appeal, along with any protection for writers a detector misreads. I can’t say whether that would change outcomes for students.
